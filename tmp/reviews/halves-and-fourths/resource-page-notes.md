# Halves and Fourths of Shapes: Resource Page Notes

Grade 1 Math. Five printable worksheets that teach children to
split circles and rectangles into equal shares, name the shares,
and compare their sizes. Equal means the parts are the same size,
not the same shape.

## What children learn

- A split shows equal parts only when every part is the same size.
- Two equal parts are halves. Two halves make the whole. Each
  part is half of the shape.
- Four equal parts are fourths. Fourths are also called quarters.
  Four fourths make the whole. Each part is a fourth, or quarter,
  of the shape.
- Equal shares can look different: halves cut up and down, across,
  or on a slant are all halves; fourths as strips or as a grid
  are all fourths.
- More equal parts means smaller pieces of the same whole: a
  fourth of a shape is smaller than a half of the same shape.

## Page by page

### Page 1: Equal Parts or Not?

A worked pair shows equal parts beside not-equal parts. Children
tick the shapes split into equal parts and cross the others,
using the response box under each shape.

Answers: circle halves, tick; circle with the off-center split,
cross (one part is bigger); rectangle halves, tick; rectangle
split into a narrow and a wide part, cross; rectangle fourths,
tick (all four strips the same size); circle fourths, cross (the
wedges are not all the same size).

### Page 2: Halves

A worked example shows a whole circle beside its two halves: 2
halves make the whole, and each part is half of the circle.
Children color 1 half of each of four shapes, split vertically,
horizontally, and on a slant.

Answers: color 1 half of each shape. Each shape is split into 2
equal parts, so coloring either half is right.

### Page 3: Fourths

A worked grid example shows one fourth colored, beside a
demonstration circle with dashed lines joining opposite dots
through the center. The explanation reads: 4 equal fourths make
one whole. Fourths are also called quarters. Children join the
guide dots to make 4 equal parts, then color 1 fourth of each
shape. The dots sit at the exact division points, so the parts
come out genuinely equal.

Answers: join opposite dots through the center (for the strips,
join the matching top and bottom dots), then color 1 fourth of
each shape.

### Page 4: Same Share, Different Look

Four pairs of identical wholes, each pair partitioned
differently. Children color one part in each shape, then tick if
both shapes show halves or both show fourths. A reminder line
reads: Equal shares can look different.

Answers: pair 1, tick (both halves, one cut up and down, one cut
across); pair 2, tick (both halves, one cut up and down, one cut
on a slant); pair 3, tick (both fourths, one as strips, one as a
2 by 2 grid); pair 4, no tick (one shape shows halves and the
other shows fourths, so they are different shares).

### Page 5: More Parts, Smaller Pieces

Two rows of identical wholes: circles, then rectangles, each
shown in halves and in fourths. Captions name each share: 1 half
of the circle, 1 quarter of the circle, 1 half of the rectangle,
1 quarter of the rectangle. Children color 1 share in each shape
and tick the smaller share in the box beneath it.

Answers: the smaller share in each row is the fourth (quarter).
The wholes are the same size, so cutting into 4 parts makes each
part smaller than cutting into 2 parts.

## Tips for grown-ups

- Let your child explain in their own words. Asking "How do you
  know?" builds the reasoning this pack teaches. Equal means the
  same size, not the same shape.
- If diagonal halves look wrong to your child, fold a paper
  circle or rectangle on the slant and show the two parts match
  exactly.
- For page 5, try it with real sharing: the same cake cut for 2
  people gives bigger pieces than the same cake cut for 4 people.
- The separate parent answer key lists every answer. Nothing on
  the child pages gives an answer away by position.
